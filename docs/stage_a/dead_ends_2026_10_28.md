# Campaign dead ends and overnight eleven-round capacity — 28 October 2026

An eleven-round self-play measurement stopped after 45 minutes. One game's
search reached a nonterminal position with no legal action. The benchmark
treated that as a fatal error and discarded all eight games, and it kept no
position, so that game's exact defect is unknown. Random play through the same
environment the search uses then found three engine defects that end games this
way. All three are fixed, the benchmark now keeps a failing game's positions
while the other games finish, and a rerun completed all ten games. The
[evidence JSON](dead_ends_2026_10_28.json) records workloads, results and
provenance; raw outputs remain under `studies/campaign/runs/dead-ends-2026-10-28/`
and `studies/campaign/runs/eleven-round-confirm-2026-10-28/`.

**Result: ten production-like eleven-round games completed in 47.5 minutes on
ten processes, 12.6 complete games per hour for one batch. Each game took 34 to
47 minutes, 41 on average, so a queue that keeps all ten processes busy plays
about 14.6 games per hour, or about 110 complete games in an eight-hour night.**

## How the defects were found

`tools/fuzz_campaign_dead_ends.py` plays seeded random eleven-round games,
choosing uniformly among legal actions and ending a phase with extra
probability 2%, 5% or 10% by seed so games reach later rounds. Each game stops
at a decision with no legal action or at an engine error and saves the
position; replaying the seed walks back to the action that caused it. A game
takes about 14 seconds, so 322 games ran in eight minutes on ten processes.

| --- | ---: | ---: |

## Defects

**Ships leaving port past Enemy warships in Non-combat Movement (14 games).**
Ships the CCP or KMT had captured in Chinese ports sailed out into a sea zone
held by Japanese warships during Non-combat Movement. The engine offered a
screening force, which declared a naval battle that Non-combat Movement can
never fight, so the phase could not end. Route checks skipped a route's first
sea zone, which a ship at sea already occupies but a ship in port still enters.
RB 11.4 forbids Surface Ships to move into a sea zone containing Enemy Surface
Warships or aircraft on MAP; RB 11.5 requires a screening force to exit port
past them, and RB 9.4.2 makes screening combat movement. Ships in port now
count their first sea zone as entered.

**Aircraft stranded in Combat Movement (4 games).** RB 9.3 requires every
attacking aircraft to have a landing spot only at the end of Combat Movement,
so the engine lets a plane fly before its Carrier arrives and checks a flight
only for a landing that could still become possible. Later legal moves can
remove that possibility. In seed 5090 a second bomber took the last free deck
of the only Carrier within an attacking bomber's reach, and the one Carrier that
could have come was used elsewhere; the phase could not end, and the other
moves ran out. At the table a player would take moves back; the engine has no
take-back. Now, when no other action is legal and only stranded aircraft keep
Combat Movement from ending, the phase may end, and the aircraft that no joint
landing assignment can include are lost before combat, chosen in unit order.
This adds a choice only where no other action exists. It gives no attack to an
aircraft that cannot land, so it cannot serve as a kamikaze attack. This is an
engineering decision, not a rulebook rule; letting the aircraft fight and then
lose them under RB 10.10 is the alternative.

**Scrambling Carrier left its aircraft in port (1 game).** A Japanese light
Carrier scrambling from Rehe's port to defend sea zone P14 moved without the
Tactical Bomber on its deck, and the state check rejected cargo and carrier in
different zones. The cargo now moves with the ship, as it already did when
scrambled ships return to port.

## Benchmark failures

A rules defect now ends only the game it reaches. The report keeps that game's
error, decision count, actual position and, when search found no legal action,
the searched position, and marks the cohort `game_error` without a
completed-game rate. Other games finish and keep their timings. In
single-process mode, games searched in one batch fail together, because one
search's failure cannot be separated within a batch.

## Overnight capacity

Production-like settings: hidden size 64, two message layers, 16,384 token
buckets, 64 simulations, depth cap 16, one thread per game, seeds 1951–1960,
ten processes, CPU only, no learning updates.

| Ten eleven-round games | Value |
| --- | ---: |
| Completed / failed | 10 / 0 |
| Batch wall time | 2,847 s |
| Complete games per hour, one batch | 12.6 |
| Seconds per game: mean, range | 2,461; 2,066–2,847 |
| Process utilization | 86% |
| Queue estimate at ten busy processes | 14.6 games per hour |
| Decisions per game | 1,139–1,455 |
| Network share of search time | 71% |
| Peak charged memory | 9.14 GB |

The batch waits for its slowest game, so its rate understates a long run. A
queue keeps processes busy until its last games, giving about 14.6 games per
hour and about 110 complete games in eight hours, less the games still running
at the end. Two earlier games recorded for the viewer took 2,800 and 2,829
seconds while ten games ran at once; recording adds disk writes and search
menus. Training adds learning updates to these times.

The network is the largest cost. Batching evaluations from all game processes
on the GPU remains the next large lever.
